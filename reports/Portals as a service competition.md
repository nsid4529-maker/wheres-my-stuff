# Start with supplier portals, sell transactions to builders

As of November 2026, "Portals As A Service" is a viable business only as a vertical transaction network. A horizontal "computer-use API for any portal" is not viable. The underlying capability is now cheap and available everywhere. Frontier models ship computer use natively at roughly **72–75% on OSWorld-Verified**, cloud browsers cost **$0.05–0.12 per browser-hour**, and the leading agent frameworks are open source. The best-known horizontal "user-agent API," **Anon, was bought by Sierra on November 5, 2026** to serve Sierra's own agents. The vertical markets split three ways. In some, the incumbents own the rails: Plaid in banking, HappyRobot in freight, and Availity, Waystar and Stedi in healthcare EDI. In others, the portal owner explicitly bans bots and regulation is pushing toward APIs, as with UnitedHealth, Cigna and Availity terms and CMS-0057-F's FHIR mandate for January 2027. The rest have demand but no neutral API layer: supplier/accounts-receivable procurement portals, insurance servicing portals, and government/DMV/permit portals. The recommended first vertical is **supplier/AR portals** (Coupa, Ariba, Jaggaer and the long tail of buyer portals). There, the supplier logs in with its own credentials, today's competitors are small AR applications that could become customers, and per-invoice pricing maps directly onto per-transaction billing. The first customers should be AR-automation, billing and receivables-finance software companies, not end-user finance teams. The plan should assume that once volume is visible, Coupa and SAP do what JPMorgan did to Plaid. The main caveat is that the AR case rests on the thinnest evidence of any vertical. No transaction-volume data was found, no funding data exists for the incumbent AR apps, and Coupa's and Ariba's supplier terms were not checked. The first 30 days must close those gaps before any build.

## Computer use is commodity infrastructure, so value has moved above it

The stack has three layers, and the bottom two are already commoditized. The first is cloud-browser infrastructure, where five or more vendors sell nearly identical hosted Chromium. **Browserbase** raised a $40M Series B at a **$300M valuation** in June 2025 and charges about $0.10–0.12 per browser-hour above its plan allowances ([AIM Media House](https://aimmediahouse.com/ai-startups/browserbase-raises-40-million-series-b-as-demand-for-ai-web-automation-accelerates); [humanbrowser.cloud](https://humanbrowser.cloud/blog/browserbase-alternative-2026)). **Kernel** raised about $22M led by Accel. **Anchor** raised a $6M seed and charges $0.05–0.09 per hour. Steel and Hyperbrowser sit around $0.08–0.10 ([SiliconANGLE](https://siliconangle.com/2025/10/22/anchor-secures-6m-build-next-generation-agentic-browser-automation/); [ecorpit](https://ecorpit.com/cloud-browsers-ai-agents-browserbase-steel-kernel-2026/)). The second layer is agent frameworks. **Browser Use** ($17M seed, Felicis) and **Skyvern** (AGPL, $29–$149/month plans) are open source ([SiliconANGLE](https://siliconangle.com/2025/03/23/browser-use-raises-17m-help-steer-ai-agents-internet/); [mediar.ai](https://www.mediar.ai/t/skyvern-pricing-official-2026)). **Integuru** takes the opposite technical route: it reverse-engineers a site's internal APIs instead of driving the UI ([YC](https://www.ycombinator.com/companies/integuru)).

The model labs set the floor for capability. OpenAI moved computer use into a `computer` tool in the Responses API on GPT-5.4, reporting **75.0% on OSWorld-Verified**. Anthropic's Claude models are reported at about **72.5–72.7%**, and Google put Gemini 2.5 Computer Use into public API preview in October 2025 ([digitalapplied](https://www.digitalapplied.com/blog/computer-use-agents-2026-claude-openai-gemini-matrix); [ecorpit](https://ecorpit.com/gemini-vs-claude-vs-openai-computer-use-browser-agent-2026/)). These come from secondary blogs, but the direction is unambiguous. The labs sell a general capability and do not maintain per-portal workflows, guarantee completion, or take liability for a mis-filed invoice. A 75% benchmark score is also far from the 98–99%+ unattended success that a paid API transaction needs. The work of closing that gap on specific portals is where a startup's margin lives.

The third layer, a managed "API for any website behind a login," is the direct analog, and it is thin. **Deck** (Montreal, about $16.5M raised) pitches "Plaid-ify any website," starting with utility portals, and **charges only per successful API call** ([TechCrunch](https://techcrunch.com/2025/04/16/deck-raises-12m-to-plaid-ify-any-website-using-ai/)). **Anon** raised more than $13M from investors including USV and built auth/credential handling, browser infrastructure and an embeddable SDK ([USV](https://www.usv.com/writing/2026/05/our-investment-in-anon-2/)). It was then absorbed by Sierra so that Sierra's customer-experience agents could work behind logins ([Dealroom](https://dealroom.co/news/170115-sierra-buys-anon-to-let-its-ai-agents-work-behind-website-logins/)). Uniphore's purchase of Orby AI in August 2025 follows the same pattern ([Seedtable](https://seedtable.com/exits/orby-ai)). Application companies buy behind-login capability and turn it into an internal feature. That cuts both ways: it removes a horizontal competitor, and it means the largest potential customers may prefer to own the capability.

Legacy RPA vendors compete through enterprise licenses, not hosted APIs. **UiPath** brought Agent Builder, Maestro, ScreenPlay (an LLM-driven UI agent) and **Healing Agent (self-repairing selectors)** on-premises in April 2026 ([UiPath IR](https://ir.uipath.com/news/detail/446/uipath-automation-suite-delivers-on-premises-agentic-ai-for-the-public-sector)). **SS&C Blue Prism** launched WorkHQ, and **Automation Anywhere** rebranded around "agentic process automation" ([Blue Prism](https://www.blueprism.com/resources/blog/agentic-automation-roadmap-2026/); [PeerSpot](https://www.peerspot.com/products/comparisons/automation-anywhere_vs_blue-prism)). They matter mainly when a large carrier, health system or enterprise AP department decides to build in-house. Self-healing selectors also weaken the old argument that RPA is too brittle to maintain.

## Vertical incumbents are opening sanctioned rails while startups build apps

**Insurance has the best-documented pain and a crowded app layer.** Independent agencies averaged **19.7 carrier appointments in 2025, up from 17.2**, and "dealing with multiple carrier interfaces" again ranked as their top technology problem ([IA Magazine](https://www.iamagazine.com/2026/10/01/evolving-channel-key-findings-from-the-2026-agency-universe-study/)). In Ivans' 2026 survey, **74% of agents named re-keying their top pain**, and **90% had cut business with a carrier over friction** ([Risk & Insurance](https://riskandinsurance.com/re-keying-and-workflow-friction-are-costing-carriers-placement-ivans-survey-finds/)). Applied Systems now controls much of the connectivity stack: Ivans, EZLynx, Tarmika, Indio and, since September 2025, Cytora ([FurtherAI](https://www.furtherai.com/blog/ai-tools-for-commercial-insurance-underwriting)). Small-commercial real-time quoting still reaches only about 30–60 carriers per platform ([QuoteSweep](https://www.quotesweep.com/blog/commercial-insurance-quoting-tools-compared)). **CoverForce** ($13M Series A from Insight Partners, about $18M total) offers one API across 60+ carriers and launched a Startup Program for new brokerages in September 2026 ([Insight Partners](https://www.insightpartners.com/ideas/coverforce-secures-13-million-in-series-a-funding-led-by-insight-partners-to-build-infrastructure-and-connectivity-between-insurance-carriers-and-agencies/); [FinTech Global](https://fintech.global/2026/10/07/coverforce-aims-to-cut-insurance-brokerage-launch-times/)).

At least five companies explicitly drive carrier portals with agents, and all of them sell finished applications. **Covera** (YC F26) fills 100+ carrier portals through a browser extension, "never submits a quote on its own," and reports 90+ brokers live in three months ([YC](https://www.ycombinator.com/companies/covera)). **Strada** launched carrier-portal browser automation on September 24, 2026 for carriers, MGAs and TPAs, naming Hippo, Clearcover and Hagerty as customers ([Newsfile](https://www.newsfilecorp.com/release/315832/Strada-Launches-Browser-Automation-for-Carrier-Portals-and-Legacy-Systems-Without-APIs)). **QuoteSweep** tracks 540+ carriers but automates quoting on only 25. Its prices run $49–$299/month, about **$3–5 per submission** ([QuoteSweep](https://www.quotesweep.com/)). Insure Stack (formerly Mulligan) produces draft quotes from portals ([YC](https://www.ycombinator.com/companies/mulligan)). Carriers are also building inside their own portals; Travelers added AI workers' comp quoting to its Travis agent portal in October 2026 ([BusinessWire](https://www.financialcontent.com/article/bizwire-2026-10-28-travelers-introduces-ai-powered-quoting-tool-for-workers-compensation)). The labor benchmark that automation must beat is offshore staffing at about **$1,500–2,800 per FTE per month** ([Helpware](https://helpware.com/blog/insurance-bpo-companies)). A neutral API for AI brokerages such as **Harper ($47M, 5,000+ customers)** is still open ([FinTech Global](https://fintech.global/2026/02/26/harper-raises-47m-to-modernise-business-insurance-with-ai/)). The weak point is post-bind servicing: endorsements, claims status, commission downloads and documents. Strada is the only notable player there, and it sells to carriers rather than agencies.

**Healthcare pays the most and is the most hostile.** Waystar shipped agentic AI three times in 2026, including autonomous denial resubmission ([TechTarget](https://www.techtarget.com/revcyclemanagement/news/366649778/Waystar-expands-AI-powered-agentic-revenue-cycle-tools)). The decisive signal is **Availity Extend (November 2026)**, which exposes REST APIs, a sandbox and **Model Context Protocol tooling for customer-managed AI agents** across coverage, claims, remittance and prior auth ([Availity](https://www.availity.com/news/availity-extend-securely-opens-the-nations-largest-health-intelligence-network-to-builders-automation-and-ai-agents/)). Availity's Essentials agreement prohibits screen scraping and web bots ([Availity OAA](https://essentials.availity.com/availity/documents/Availity_OAA.pdf)). UnitedHealth Group's and HealthPartners' terms bar automated scraping without written agreement ([UHG](https://www.unitedhealthgroup.com/terms-of-use.html); [HealthPartners](https://www.healthpartners.com/provider-public/legal/terms-of-use/)). **Stedi** prices 270/271 eligibility checks at **$0.30 falling to $0.08**, which makes basic eligibility a losing fight for a portal agent ([Stedi](https://www.stedi.com/pricing)). The AI-native field is heavily funded: Tennr raised a $101M Series C at $605M ([MobiHealthNews](https://www.mobihealthnews.com/news/tennr-openai-test-ai-agents-prior-authorization)), Latent Health has an $80M Series A at about $600M with more than $20M in revenue ([Forbes](https://www.forbes.com/sites/rashishrivastava/2026/03/18/this-600-million-ai-startup-is-speeding-up-paperwork-so-patients-get-drugs-faster/)), and Silna, Basata and Infinitus are active. **Asteroid** (YC W25), a "HIPAA compliant API" for 100+ payer portals and EHRs, and **Clicks Health** (YC F25) already occupy the "Stedi for portals" slot ([asteroid.ai](https://asteroid.ai/); [YC](https://www.ycombinator.com/companies/industry/health-tech)). The prize is real. Medical prior auth is still only **40% electronic**, and a manual PA costs about **$11–14** ([AJMC](https://www.ajmc.com/view/caqh-index-finds-20-billion-in-cost-savings-opportunities); [Becker's](https://www.beckershospitalreview.com/finance/what-8-manual-tasks-cost-the-healthcare-industry-per-transaction/)). A small team would arrive late and face explicit ToS bans.

**Freight and banking are closed to a new API layer.** HappyRobot closed a **$150M Series C at a $1.2B valuation** in August 2026, with 8 of the 10 largest US freight brokers running its agents, and it is expanding into utilities, telecom and P&C insurance ([FreightWaves](https://www.freightwaves.com/news/happyrobot-series-c-freighttech-unicorn); [Supply Chain Digital](https://supplychaindigital.com/news/happyrobot-raises-150m-in-funding-to-grow-agent-deployment)). Vooma, Pallet, Parade and Evos fill the rest of the space. **Freight Hero** charges a flat fee per load, which confirms per-transaction willingness to pay ([PR Newswire](https://www.prnewswire.com/news-releases/freight-hero-lands-5m-to-prove-freight-brokerage-needs-fewer-tools-more-outcomes-302835207.html)). In banking, Plaid (valued at about $8B, with an estimated **$546M ARR**) and the other aggregators signed fee contracts with JPMorgan covering more than **95% of third-party data pulls** on its systems ([Sacra](https://sacra.com/research/plaid-at-546m-arr-growing-40-yoy/); [CNBC](https://www.cnbc.com/2025/11/14/jpmorgan-chase-fintech-fees.html)). The CFPB's Section 1033 rule is enjoined and being rewritten ([Cozen O'Connor](https://www.cozen.com/news-resources/publications/2026/section-1033-compliance-date-open-banking-rule-enjoined-and-under-reconsideration)). **Payroll and HR** connectivity is already sold as an API, by Finch at **$65 per connection per month** with 40,000+ employers connected, and by Pinwheel, Argyle, Atomic, Noyo and Ideon ([Finch](https://tryfinch.com/pricing); [Finch support](https://support.tryfinch.com/articles/5195481049-what-is-finch)).

**Government and supplier portals are where no neutral API layer exists.** On the government side, **PermitFlow** raised a $54M Series B from Accel (about $90M total) for construction permitting, with Amazon, Ikea and Lennar as named customers ([BusinessWire](https://www.businesswire.com/news/home/20251202551013/en)). Verra Mobility launched AI title and registration for fleets in September 2026 ([PR Newswire](https://www.prnewswire.com/news-releases/verra-mobility-launches-ai-driven-title--registration-solution-cutting-fleet-vehicle-activation-time-up-to-80-302867237.html)), and Fairway and Cario handle AI titling ([Cario](https://cario.com/ai-titling-agent/)). On the supplier side, **Tesorio** markets agents that log into Coupa and Ariba to submit invoices, track payment and download remittance ([Tesorio](https://www.tesorio.com/product/supplier-portal)). Invoice Butler, Lunos and LedgerUp do the same, with no disclosed funding ([Invoice Butler](https://www.invoicebutler.com/blog/ar-automation-tools-customer-payment-portals); [Lunos](https://www.lunos.ai/blog/coupa-supplier-portal-invoice-submission); [LedgerUp](https://www.ledgerup.ai/)). Coupa's supplier portal is free for suppliers, while **SAP Ariba already charges suppliers** to invoice through its network, and both reject invoices that fail strict PO matching and line-item formatting ([Transalis](https://www.transalis.com/blogs/supplier-portal-invoicing)). Those rejections are the source of the pain.

## Seven threats, and which verticals survive them

**Commoditization by model labs and infra.** This threat is already here and fatal for horizontal plays. Any team with Browser Use, a Browserbase account and a frontier model can make one portal work in a week. The analysts' verdict that "thin wrappers around generic APIs" are indefensible applies directly ([thinkia](https://thinkia.com/thoughts/enterprise-ai-strategy-the-new-moat-beyond-the-api/)). What survives is what labs will not build. That means a normalized cross-portal schema (invoice, status and remittance objects that look the same whether they came from Coupa or a county permit site), idempotent transaction semantics with receipts and retries, a maintained library of portal workflows with monitoring, and compliance artifacts such as SOC 2, credential vaulting and audit logs. Verticals with a single dominant portal suffer most, because a customer can build against one portal itself. Verticals with deep long tails, such as hundreds of buyer portals, dozens of carrier portals or thousands of jurisdictions, are the most robust.

**Portal owners blocking or enforcing ToS.** This is the threat that most separates the verticals. In healthcare it is explicit and documented: Availity, UHG, Cigna and HealthPartners all prohibit bots without written approval, and Availity says it "decided to eliminate bot access" ([Availity blog](https://www.availity.com/blog/why-apis-are-better-than-bots/)). The legal backdrop improved on August 4, 2026, when the Ninth Circuit vacated Amazon's injunction against Perplexity's Comet agent and held that **the human user, not the agent developer, "accesses" the site** under the CFAA. The court limited the ruling to its record and left contract and ToS claims open ([ABA](https://www.americanbar.org/groups/litigation/resources/newsletters/privacy-data-security/ninth-circuit-holds-human-user-not-developer-ai-agent-responsible-websites-accessed/); [WSGR](https://www.wsgr.com/en/insights/ninth-circuit-addresses-cfaa-and-agentic-ai-tools-in-groundbreaking-decision.html)). The criminal-law risk has fallen. The account-termination risk has not. The safest posture is for the account owner, acting on its own account, to direct each transaction. Supplier portals fit that best, because the supplier is the invited user of a portal the buyer provisioned for it. Government filings fit next. Insurance agencies fit with caveats, since commentary says portal terms tie access to named individuals and that shared logins break carrier IT requirements ([Peakflo](https://peakflo.co/blog/ai-browser-agent-mfa-captcha-session-timeouts-vendor-portals); [Atticus Rowan](https://atticusrowan.com/blog/insurance-agency-it-carrier-requirements)). No carrier ToS text or enforcement case was found. Cloudflare's agent-blocking defaults, in effect since September 15, 2026, mainly hit ad-supported consumer pages rather than authenticated B2B portals ([o-mega](https://o-mega.ai/articles/cloudflare-blocks-ai-agents-by-default-2026)).

**MFA and credential handling.** This is the largest operational problem and the clearest place to build a moat. When MFA fires, a browser agent needs a human to relay the code. If the code goes to a named employee's phone, the arrangement looks like impersonation rather than a service identity ([Peakflo](https://peakflo.co/blog/ai-browser-agent-mfa-captcha-session-timeouts-vendor-portals)). Anon's whole stack centered on this problem, which is part of why Sierra bought it ([USV](https://www.usv.com/writing/2026/05/our-investment-in-anon-2/)). The workable design gives each customer user its own vaulted credentials, a dedicated MFA channel (a shared inbox or TOTP seed the customer provisions to a service account where the portal allows it), session persistence, and a human-approval relay as fallback. Verticals where portal accounts belong to a company rather than a licensed individual (supplier portals, many permit portals) handle this better than insurance, where portal access is tied to individual producers, or healthcare.

**Regulatory API mandates.** These hit healthcare hardest and on a fixed date. CMS-0057-F requires Medicare Advantage, Medicaid/CHIP and federal-exchange plans to stand up FHIR prior-authorization APIs by **January 1, 2027**, and no delay had been announced as of September 2026 ([CMS](https://www.cms.gov/files/document/fact-sheet-cms-interoperability-and-prior-authorization-final-rule-cms-0057-f.pdf)). About 50 insurers separately pledged real-time answers for at least **80% of complete electronic PAs in 2027** ([AAMC](https://www.aamc.org/advocacy-policy/washington-highlights/health-plans-announce-voluntary-commitment-prior-authorization)). The window does not close at once, because commercial/ERISA plans, drugs, DME and dental carve-outs, utilization-management vendors, appeals and credentialing all fall outside the mandate, and provider-side EHR adoption lags. But a startup that builds on big-payer PA portals is building on ground the regulator is removing. Banking's 1033 rule points the same way, just more slowly. Insurance, supplier and government portals face no comparable mandate.

**Incumbents shipping APIs or charging for access (the "JPMorgan moment").** Plaid's history is the template. The scraping phase was a bridge. Once volume was visible, the data owner charged for access, at an original proposal of **up to $300M a year** for Plaid, before settling lower ([CNBC](https://www.cnbc.com/2025/11/14/jpmorgan-chase-fintech-fees.html)). The equivalents are already visible elsewhere: Availity Extend in healthcare, Applied's Ivans download pipe and carrier-specific integrations such as Travelers' submissionless renewal in insurance ([Insurance Business](https://www.insurancebusinessmag.com/us/news/technology/applied-systems-launches-submissionless-renewal-experience-with-travelers-581797.aspx)), and Ariba's existing supplier fees in procurement. The right response is to plan to become the sanctioned partner. Noyo and Plaid did this by aggregating many counterparties behind one schema and then negotiating formal pipes ([Founder-led sales stories](https://founderledsalesstories.substack.com/p/from-pdfs-to-apis-how-noyo-built)). An "API-first, portal-fallback" architecture, which routes through official APIs where they exist and agents everywhere else, turns this threat into a feature.

**Reliability.** This kills projects quietly. Customers pay per transaction only if success is near-certain and failure is explicit. No vendor publishes flake or MFA-failure rates, and the notes found no independent complaint data. That is an evidence gap, and it is also the main thing a startup can win on. Covera's and QuoteSweep's human-in-the-loop positioning ("never submits on its own") suggests that in insurance, errors-and-omissions exposure keeps final binding with humans. A per-transaction API should therefore expose a clear state machine (`prepared`, `submitted`, `confirmed`, `rejected`, `needs_human`) and should charge only for confirmed outcomes, as Deck does.

**Acquisitions and customers building in-house.** Sierra–Anon and Uniphore–Orby show that the most valuable customers can also buy the capability. Strada building carrier-portal automation in-house shows they can also build it. This risk is highest where the customer's core product *is* the portal work: Tesorio's supplier portal module, or Covera's quoting. It is lowest where portal execution is a side requirement, as for billing platforms, ERPs, receivables-finance lenders, AI brokerages that would rather spend engineering on distribution, and construction software that needs permit status. An acquisition is also the most likely good exit, so building something an app company would want to buy is a reasonable plan.

## Supplier portals score highest once blocking risk is priced in

The scores below run from 1 to 5 and are judgments synthesized from the evidence above, not measurements. For Competition and Blocking/legal risk, a higher score is worse. The composite adds Pain, Volume and WTP to the inverted Competition and Risk scores (6 minus the score), for a maximum of 25.

| Vertical | Pain | Volume | WTP | Competition (5 = crowded) | Blocking/legal risk (5 = severe) | Composite /25 | Verdict |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Supplier/AR procurement portals** | 4 | 5 | 4 | 2 | 2 | **21** | **First vertical** |
| Insurance servicing (endorsements, COIs, claims status, commissions) | 4 | 4 | 3 | 3 | 3 | 17 | Strong second; sell to AI brokerages |
| Government / DMV title / permits | 4 | 3 | 3 | 3 | 2 | 17 | Good long-tail fit; slow per-jurisdiction volume |
| Insurance quoting / new business | 5 | 4 | 3 | 4 | 3 | 17 | Pain proven, app layer crowded |
| Healthcare big-payer PA / eligibility / claims | 5 | 5 | 5 | 5 | 5 | 17 | Avoid: banned, mandated away, funded incumbents |
| Freight / logistics | 4 | 5 | 4 | 5 | 3 | 17 | Avoid as first vertical: HappyRobot dominance |
| Healthcare niches (dental, credentialing, UM vendors, DME) | 4 | 3 | 4 | 3 | 4 | 16 | Later expansion via RCM customers |
| Payroll / HR / benefits | 3 | 4 | 4 | 4 | 3 | 16 | Already API-ified (Finch et al.) |
| Utilities | 3 | 3 | 3 | 3 | 2 | 16 | Deck is already there |
| Banking data | 4 | 5 | 4 | 5 | 5 | 15 | Closed |

Five verticals tie at 17, which is the honest result. Each has a real disqualifier that a simple sum hides. Healthcare's high pain and willingness to pay are cancelled by explicit bot bans and a January 2027 mandate. Freight's volume is cancelled by a $1.2B incumbent. Insurance quoting's pain is cancelled by a crowded field: Covera, QuoteSweep, Insure Stack, CoverForce and Applied's raters. Supplier/AR leads because none of its scores is a disqualifier. Its scores also rest on the weakest evidence in this research. No invoice-volume data was found, the funding and traction of Tesorio's portal product and the smaller AR apps are unknown, and **whether Coupa's and Ariba's supplier terms prohibit automated access was not checked**. If those terms ban automation and are enforced, the vertical's risk score rises to 4 and insurance servicing becomes the first choice.

Insurance servicing is the right fallback, and possibly the better choice for a team with insurance relationships. The pain is the best-documented in the research (74% of agents name re-keying, an average of 19.7 appointments). The servicing workflow has less agency-side AI competition than quoting. AI-native brokerages such as Harper are natural API customers that would rather not build portal agents for dozens of carriers. Its weaknesses are per-producer credential terms, the fact that Applied's Ivans download pipe already covers some policy and commission data, and that Strada is already in the market on the carrier side.

## A 90-day path to product-market fit for an invoice-portal API

**First customer: software companies that need portal execution but don't sell it.** Selling directly to supplier finance teams would make this an AR application competing with Tesorio and Invoice Butler, and it would require a sales motion a small team cannot afford. The first customers should instead be companies whose own customers need invoices delivered to enterprise buyer portals. That includes billing and invoicing platforms, vertical ERPs and accounting tools, AR-automation vendors that lack a portal module, and receivables-finance companies (factoring, invoice lending), which need confirmed invoice status and remittance to underwrite. The healthcare research points the same way: vertical AI apps and outsourcers are better customers for a horizontal execution layer than for a competing app. Outsourcers matter here too. Offshore AR and back-office teams paid per FTE can use the API to cut per-transaction labor, much as Patra's insurance VA model prices at about **$16.7/hour** ([Patra](https://www.patracorp.com/insurance-operations-services/patra-virtual-assistant/)). The 90 days should include one or two direct "managed" suppliers with very high volume, kept deliberately, to generate transaction data and failure cases.

**Product scope: three verbs on five portals, done to SLA.** The v1 should offer `submit_invoice` (with PO-match validation *before* submission, because rejections are the pain), `get_invoice_status`, and `get_remittance`. It should run on Coupa, SAP Ariba and three more buyer portals chosen from design partners' actual volume. The deliverables that matter are the normalized invoice, status and remittance schema, webhooks on state changes, an explicit `needs_human` state with a review queue, and credential vaulting with a customer-provisioned MFA channel. An API-first router should use official supplier integration channels where a buyer offers them. The v1 should not include a dashboard for end users, quoting, a horizontal "any portal" SDK, or any portal whose terms prohibit automation without a written agreement.

**Pricing: charge only for confirmed outcomes.** The model should be a small monthly fee per connected supplier–buyer portal account (Finch's **$65 per connection per month** is the market-tested upper anchor), plus a per-transaction fee charged only for confirmed outcomes, as Deck does. The specific numbers are hypotheses to test. They are not supported by AR labor-cost data, which the research did not find. A reasonable starting grid is **$1.50–3.00 per confirmed invoice submission** and **$0.10–0.50 per status or remittance pull**, with volume tiers and a monthly minimum. Comparable sourced price points support the shape of this grid. Portal agents in insurance land at about $3–5 per submission. EDI-based healthcare lookups cost $0.08–0.30, and manual healthcare transactions cost $8–14 ([QuoteSweep](https://www.quotesweep.com/); [Stedi](https://www.stedi.com/pricing); [Becker's](https://www.beckershospitalreview.com/finance/what-8-manual-tasks-cost-the-healthcare-industry-per-transaction/)). Unit cost at $0.05–0.12 per browser-hour plus model tokens leaves room for 70%+ gross margin, *if* human-review rates stay low. The human-review rate is the single number that determines whether the business works.

**Go-to-market: founder-led sales to roughly 40 software companies, then distribution partnerships.** The pitch is "add procurement-portal delivery to your product in a week, and pay only when the invoice is accepted." Developer docs, a sandbox and an open status page showing per-portal success rates will build more trust than any sales collateral. The partnership track starts early. Once transaction volume through a portal is meaningful, approach that portal owner with clean, PO-matched submissions, to become the sanctioned integrator before it decides to charge or block. The insurance research suggests the same move: carriers have reason to welcome agents that bring them cleaner submissions.

**What the first 90 days must prove.** Days 1–30 resolve the evidence gaps. That means legal review of Coupa, Ariba and Jaggaer supplier terms and of five design partners' buyer portals; 25–30 interviews with billing, ERP, AR and receivables-finance companies; and a measured baseline of each partner's current cost and rejection rate per portal invoice. Days 31–60 put three to five design partners in production on the first portals. The aim is to measure unattended success rate, human-review rate, MFA interruptions per 100 transactions and maintenance engineer-hours per portal per month. Days 61–90 convert at least three partners to paid contracts at the target price, and add two more portals using only the maintenance tooling, which tests whether coverage scales sub-linearly with headcount.

| Kill or pivot criterion (by day 90) | Threshold | If triggered |
|---|---|---|
| Portal ToS | Coupa or Ariba supplier terms prohibit automation and partners report enforcement | Pivot to insurance servicing for AI brokerages |
| Reliability | Unattended confirmed-success rate below 95% on the top two portals after 60 days | Stop; the business cannot be priced per outcome |
| Human-review load | More than 10% of transactions need a human | Re-price as managed service, or stop |
| Maintenance | More than ~20 engineer-hours per portal per month to stay green | Coverage cannot scale; narrow or stop |
| Willingness to pay | Fewer than 3 of ~10 qualified prospects sign paid terms at ≥$1 per confirmed submission | Customers see it as a feature; stop or pivot |
| Build vs buy | Prospects say they will build it themselves with Browser Use / Skyvern | The schema/SLA moat is not valued; stop |

## Against home-services voice, portals trade speed for open space

The earlier home-services AI voice opportunity has stronger demand evidence and a clearer end buyer. The portal opportunity has a more open competitive lane and a more durable potential moat, but much weaker evidence. In home services, capital has piled into the enterprise ServiceTitan tier: **Avoca at a $1B valuation**, Netic at $450M, Probook with $40M, and Broccoli with $52M ([Avoca](https://avoca.ai/blog/avoca-raises-125m-series-b-1b-valuation); [Bloomberg](https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2025-11-13/founders-fund-backs-startup-bringing-ai-to-plumbers-and-roofers)). Field-service platforms give basic answering away, with Jobber's at **$29/month**. Hatch's sale to Yelp at about **11x ARR** shows that buyers will pay for booked outcomes ([Yelp 8-K](https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/1345016/000134501626000011/yelpq42025ex992lettertos.htm)). That wedge, a per-booked-job "revenue CSR" for mid-size contractors off ServiceTitan, would generate revenue faster. It sells to owners who feel lost calls immediately, and it competes in a market where voice quality is already sold wholesale. The portal wedge would generate revenue more slowly. It sells to software companies on API trust, and its key risks (portal ToS, Coupa or SAP monetizing access) are binary rather than gradual. But it has no $1B incumbent, its moat (normalized schema, maintained coverage, sanctioned partnerships) compounds, and its acquirers are visibly paying for this capability. A team that is infrastructure- and developer-oriented should take the portal route in supplier/AR. A team that is sales-led and wants revenue within a quarter should take home-services voice.

## Conclusion

The Sierra–Anon deal is the most informative data point in this research. It shows that "an agent that works behind logins" is a feature that application companies will buy, not a standalone category. The market will not pay a premium for operating a browser. It will pay for whatever makes a portal transaction behave like an API call: a guaranteed outcome, a consistent schema, receipts, and eventually a sanctioned relationship with the portal owner. Healthcare is instructive in the other direction. The highest-value portal work faces regulators and incumbents (CMS-0057-F, Availity Extend) actively replacing it with sanctioned APIs, which is the end state any portal business should aim for, ideally with itself as the sanctioned partner.

The most useful reframing is to treat portal automation as a temporary bridge to a negotiated data pipe, as Plaid's history shows. Supplier/AR portals are attractive because they are less likely to be blocked, and because the account owner has a strong interest in the transaction succeeding. That sets up a clean partnership pitch to Coupa- and Ariba-style platforms once volume exists. Whether the plan holds depends on two things no secondary source can settle: what the supplier terms actually say, and whether the human-review rate can be held under 10%. Both can be tested within 60 days at little cost.
